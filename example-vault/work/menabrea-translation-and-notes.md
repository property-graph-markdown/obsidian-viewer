# Menabrea Translation and Notes A–G

Ada Lovelace translated Luigi Menabrea's French description of the Analytical Engine into English during a nine-month period in 1842–1843. She added seven Notes, A through G, which together were about three times as long as the source text. The complete work was published under her initials AAL in 1843.[^ada-notes]

Ada wrote the English translation and her Notes; they were published together in 1843.[^ada-notes] [Ada Lovelace](../people/ada-lovelace.md "{type: authored_by, year: 1843, role: \"translation and notes\"}")

The source paper was written by Luigi Menabrea.[^ada-notes] [Luigi Menabrea](../people/luigi-menabrea.md "{type: based_on_work_by, original_year: 1842}")

The Notes developed through Ada's exchange with Charles Babbage. According to Babbage's later account, he supplied the mathematical formulas for Note G; Ada turned them into a step-by-step table and found an error.[^note-g-overview] [Charles Babbage](../people/charles-babbage.md "{type: developed_with, year: 1843}")

The publication contains the individually modeled [Note G](note-g.md "{type: contains}").

## Note G and its classification as a program

Note G describes a procedure for calculating Bernoulli numbers with the Analytical Engine. It is often called the first published computer program. “First published” is the careful formulation: Babbage had recorded earlier programs for the Engine, but they had not been published.[^ada-program]

## A broader idea

The Notes do more than explain a calculation. Ada argued that a programmable machine might operate on rule-bound representations such as musical structures. This concept of broader symbolic processing is central to the publication's historical significance.[^note-g-overview]
